# Architecture Decisions -- CreditGraph

*How I chose the tools, why those choices matter*

---

## Why a graph database for credit risk

The moment that convinced me was circular guarantees. Person A guarantees Company B's loan. Company B's owner guarantees Person A's loan. In SQL, finding this at depth 2 requires a self-join. At depth 3, another join. At arbitrary depth, you need recursive CTEs where you decide the maximum traversal depth at query-write time -- before you know the data. In Cypher, it is one pattern match: `(a)-[:GARANTIZA*]->(p)<-[:TIENE_PRESTAMO]-(a)`. The depth is discovered at runtime, not hardcoded by the developer. This is not a style preference or a technology fashion statement. It is a computational fact. The relationship structure IS the query, and the query adapts to whatever depth the data actually contains.

The 2008 parallel made this even clearer to me. Lehman Brothers had 7,000 legal entities spread across 40 countries. When the firm collapsed, regulators could not trace the exposure chains -- they did not know who owed what to whom through which intermediary. That failure is the reason GLEIF exists today: a global registry so that "who owns whom" is no longer buried in spreadsheets. A graph database makes those ownership and guarantee chains first-class data. You do not derive the chain from a series of table joins; the chain is stored directly as a traversal path. When I explain this in an interview, I am not arguing that graphs are better than relational databases in general. I am arguing that for THIS specific problem -- tracing correlated credit exposure through ownership and guarantee structures at unknown depth -- the graph paradigm eliminates an entire class of query complexity that SQL forces you to manage manually.

## Why PySpark instead of pandas for the ETL

The data is 1,150 rows. Pandas handles that in milliseconds. I could have written the entire ETL in 40 lines of pandas and moved on. So why did I choose PySpark? Because the ETL notebook is not just about cleaning this data. It is about demonstrating that I think in production patterns. The anti-join pattern I use for foreign key validation works identically on 1,000 rows or 100 million rows. The explicit StructType schemas catch type drift whether I am reading 5 CSVs or 5,000. The lazy evaluation model means the same transformation code runs on a single-node Databricks Community Edition cluster today and a 20-node production cluster tomorrow without a single rewrite.

What I actually learned was what NOT to do. No UDFs, because they break Catalyst optimization and force row-by-row Python execution inside a JVM-based engine. No `.collect()` inside validation loops, because that pulls the entire dataset to the driver node's memory and will crash on anything larger than what fits in RAM. No `inferSchema=True`, because it silently guesses types and makes decisions you cannot audit. These are production anti-patterns that are completely invisible on small data -- your 1,000-row pipeline runs perfectly, you deploy it on 50 million rows, and it fails in ways that are painful to debug. Writing the ETL in PySpark on tiny data forced me to internalize the discipline before the stakes are real. The code is verbose compared to pandas, but every line of that verbosity exists for a reason that matters at scale.

## Why Spark MLlib GBT and what calibration adds on top

I initially considered LightGBM, which is the industry standard for tabular credit data. But LightGBM is a Python library -- it requires `.toPandas()` to leave the Spark execution engine, and the moment you do that, you lose the distributed pipeline. For a 300-row dataset, this is irrelevant. For the interview, it matters: the question is whether the pipeline stays native Spark end-to-end or breaks into a pandas detour midway. Spark MLlib's GBTClassifier handles mixed feature types through VectorAssembler, and the entire chain -- assembly, training, prediction, calibration -- stays inside the Spark DAG. The model selection itself was the straightforward part. The decision that actually matters is what happens AFTER the model produces scores.

A LightGBM model outputs scores that rank borrowers by risk. A score of 0.6 means "riskier than someone at 0.3." It does NOT mean "60% probability of default." This distinction is invisible in most Kaggle competitions, where all you need is correct ranking (AUC). But for expected loss provisioning -- where the formula is EL = PD x EAD x LGD -- you need actual probabilities, not rankings. If the model says 0.6 and the true probability is 0.04, your provision estimate is off by a factor of 15. That is where Platt scaling enters: it fits a logistic regression on a held-out calibration set to map raw scores to calibrated probabilities that match observed default rates across score bins.

I chose a three-way split deliberately: 60% train, 20% calibration, 20% test. Most tutorials I found use two-way splits and either calibrate on training data (which overfits the calibrator to the same data the model memorized) or calibrate on test data (which contaminates the only evaluation set you have). The held-out calibration set keeps everything honest -- the model never sees the calibration data during training, and the test set is never touched until final evaluation. This is where the actuarial training shows up. The distinction between discrimination (can you rank-order risks?) and calibration (are your probability estimates accurate?) is foundational in credibility theory and loss reserving. An actuary who builds a model that ranks well but miscalibrates expected losses by 15x has built a model that looks great in development and destroys reserves in production.

## Why predetermined Cypher queries in the AI layer

The GraphRAG layer routes natural language questions to predetermined Cypher query templates instead of letting the LLM generate Cypher on the fly. This was a deliberate architectural constraint, not a limitation I could not work around.

I tested LLM-generated Cypher early in the project. The results were instructive. The model would reverse traversal directions -- writing `(a)<-[:GARANTIZA]-(p)` when the schema requires `(a)-[:GARANTIZA]->(p)` -- producing syntactically valid queries that silently return empty results or wrong results. It would invent property names that sound reasonable but do not exist in the schema. It would produce queries that look right, return numbers, and those numbers would be wrong. In most software applications, a wrong answer triggers an obvious error. In credit risk, a query that returns exposure of 5 million when the real answer is 50 million is the most dangerous kind of bug: it looks correct, it passes review, and it drives provisioning decisions in the wrong direction.

Predetermined queries solve this completely. I wrote 15 Cypher templates covering the core question types: exposure aggregation, contagion cascades, circular guarantee detection, hidden concentration, single points of failure. A risk officer can review those 15 templates, verify that each one matches the business logic it claims to implement, and sign off once. The LLM's job becomes classification -- "which of these 15 templates matches the user's natural language question?" -- not generation. Classification is something LLMs are genuinely good at. Arbitrary code generation in a domain-specific query language with strict directional semantics is something they are not. I chose the architecture that plays to the model's actual strengths.

## Why Parquet and explicit schemas

These are small decisions that signal whether someone has dealt with production data pipelines or only with tutorial datasets. I chose Parquet over CSV because it provides columnar storage, built-in compression, schema enforcement at the file level, and predicate pushdown for Spark reads. But the more important signal is the choice to define explicit StructType schemas rather than relying on `inferSchema=True`.

Here is what `inferSchema` actually does: it reads the file twice -- once to scan values and guess types, once to load the data using those guesses. If a column contains "123" in every row, it becomes IntegerType. If one row in ten thousand contains "N/A", the entire column silently becomes StringType, or worse, the "N/A" silently becomes null and you lose the information that a record had an invalid value. By defining schemas explicitly -- with dates as StringType that I cast after validation, with amounts as DoubleType that I range-check after loading -- I control exactly when and how type coercion happens. The 127 quality issues I embedded in the raw data exist precisely to demonstrate this point. Negative incomes, invalid dates, duplicate CURPs, orphaned foreign keys -- these are exact bugs that bank data teams find on a weekly basis. If `inferSchema` silently converts an invalid date to null, I lose the ability to report WHY a record failed validation. The explicit schema preserves the raw mess so that the ETL can document every rejection with a reason.

## Why real ownership topology instead of fully synthetic data

I could have generated the entire graph synthetically. A Barabasi-Albert model produces power-law degree distributions that resemble real ownership networks. An Erdos-Renyi model with the right parameters produces similar density. On paper, the graph statistics would look reasonable. But the topology would be wrong in ways that matter specifically for credit risk analysis.

Real ownership structures have shared directors -- the same person sits on three company boards, creating hidden linkages that do not appear in any single company's records. They have nested subsidiaries with specific majority and minority stakes. They have concentrated controllers who own 75-100% of multiple entities through different legal structures. These patterns are what create the correlated exposure that the graph is built to find. A random generator might produce a node with degree 9, but it will not produce the specific pattern of one person controlling 9 companies, 7 of which share a co-controller who appears to be related to the first person based on matching surnames and proximate birth dates. That pattern is real in the UK PSC data because it reflects how real business families operate.

I chose a hybrid approach. The UK PSC data gives real topology: who controls whom, at what ownership band (25-50%, 50-75%, 75-100%), with what nature of control (shares, voting rights, board appointment). The GLEIF data gives real Mexican corporate hierarchies: BBVA Mexico with its actual subsidiaries, Grupo Bimbo with its actual structure, Banco Inbursa as it actually exists. The synthetic layer sits on top: Mexican identities replacing UK names, loans calibrated to Mexican benchmarks, guarantees following the real ownership paths, defaults at the 4% NPL rate that CNBV publishes for conservative institutional lenders. The graph questions -- contagion depth, hidden concentration, circular guarantees -- depend on structural patterns that must be real to produce meaningful demonstrations. The credit questions -- default probability, loss given default, provision amounts -- can be synthetic without weakening the argument, because those numbers are there to show the method, not to make auditable claims.

## Why topological metrics do not replace traditional credit metrics

It would have been tempting to frame this project as "graph metrics are better than bureau scores." That framing is wrong, and making that claim would immediately signal to an interviewer that I do not understand the problem space deeply enough.

A bureau score tells you how likely THIS specific borrower is to default based on THEIR individual credit history: payment patterns, utilization ratios, length of credit history, recent inquiries. A graph metric -- PageRank, betweenness centrality, degree -- tells you something entirely different: how much damage THIS borrower's default would cause to the rest of the portfolio based on THEIR connections. These are complementary questions, not competing answers. A borrower with a perfect 850 bureau score who personally guarantees 12 loans across 4 companies he controls is low individual risk but high systemic risk. If he defaults, the cascade through those guarantees is massive despite his excellent personal history. Conversely, a borrower with a 550 score and no guarantees, no directorships, no ownership stakes, is high individual risk but zero systemic risk. Their default affects exactly one loan.

I chose an architecture that uses both paradigms for their respective strengths. The LightGBM model consumes traditional features -- bureau score, declared income, days past due, loan-to-value ratio -- to estimate individual probability of default. The graph consumes topological features -- guarantee chain depth, number of shared directorships, control concentration ratio, betweenness centrality -- to estimate how defaults correlate and propagate across the portfolio. Expected loss at the portfolio level requires both: how likely each individual default is, AND how each default propagates through the ownership and guarantee structure. Neither paradigm alone gives you the full picture. The actuarial instinct here is diversification: just as you do not put all reserves into one asset class, you do not answer all risk questions with one analytical paradigm.

## Tying it together

Every decision in this project traces back to one idea: credit risk is a network phenomenon, and the tools you choose should reflect that. The graph database stores relationships as first-class data. PySpark ensures the pipeline scales when the data does. LightGBM with Platt calibration produces actual probabilities, not just rankings. Predetermined Cypher keeps the AI layer auditable. Explicit schemas preserve the information needed to explain every rejection. Real topology makes the structural patterns meaningful. And the combination of individual and topological metrics gives a more complete picture than either alone.

This is not a production system. It is a working prototype that demonstrates a method. The honest claim is: swap the synthetic credit layer for a real loan book, keep the graph schema and queries, and the analysis transfers. The structure is real. The method is sound. The numbers are illustrative. That is what I built, and that is what I am prepared to defend.
